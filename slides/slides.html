Distributional test · SEC filing data

Do companies bend earnings
to avoid reporting a loss?

If earnings were unmanaged, the cross-firm distribution of net income should be smooth. There is no economic reason for a break at exactly zero. A manager near a small loss has discretion — so does the distribution show a notch?

Method

Predict each bin from its neighbours

expected_i = (count_{i−1} + count_{i+1}) / 2, standardized with the binomial variance. ROA = NetIncome_t / TotalAssets_{t−1} — beginning-of-year assets, always.

Three commitments, made before looking at any output and enforced in code:

  • Zero on a bin boundary, never inside a bin — every edge is an integer multiple of the bin width, unit-tested.
  • All three bin widths reported (0.0025, 0.005, 0.01). A result at one width is not a result.
  • Both sides reported — deficit below zero and surplus above. Finding only one is weaker evidence.
The headline
Robustness 1 — bin width

The direction holds. The significance does not.

Fewer small losses and more small profits than neighbours predict, at every width — the predicted sign. But the deficit below zero is never significant, and the surplus above sits right at the 0.05 boundary.

Size terciles show no concentration in small firms (z = −0.25 / −0.26 / −0.91), and the result is flat across size floors from $0 to $500m.

Revenue scaling produces a clean, significant notch at all three bin widths. Assets and book equity produce nothing. The result is a property of the scaling choice as much as of the earnings — which is exactly what Durtschi & Easton (2005) argued, arriving here empirically.

Robustness 3 — the placebo
Bottom line

No clear discontinuity — and a useful reason why

  • I did not find significant evidence of a deficit of small losses in recent SEC filing data. The direction matches the classical result at every bin width; the magnitude does not reach significance.
  • The one significant version is denominator-specific. Revenue scaling gives a clean notch; assets and book equity do not.
  • The placebo undercuts the null itself. Neighbour-averaging finds a significant "discontinuity" in cash flow, which cannot have been managed into shape — and it grows with bin width, the signature of density curvature, not a knife-edge break.
  • Where a notch appears, "consistent with earnings management" is as far as the evidence reaches. This is a distributional anomaly, not a causal finding.
  • Caveats: pooled firm-years (5.8 per firm) overstate precision; period is 2016–2025 only, so a faded post-SOX notch is compatible with the original result rather than a refutation of it.
